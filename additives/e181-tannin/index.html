Tannin

What it is

Natural astringent polyphenols found in tree bark, tea, and grapes, used as a clarifying agent and flavor modifier.

How it is used in food

Food-grade tannins (usually tannic acid from oak galls, chestnut or quebracho) are used mainly as clarifying and fining agents: they bind and precipitate haze-forming proteins in wine, beer and juice, and stabilise colour in red wine. They also add astringency and structure to beverages, and act as antioxidants that slow oxidative browning.

Commonly found in
  • Wine clarification
  • Beer processing
  • Flavorings

Health and safety

Completely safe and consumed widely in tea and wine. It acts as an antioxidant but can inhibit iron absorption in high amounts.

Long-standing safe dietary use, with intakes from tea, wine and coffee far exceeding additive use. The one real caution is nutritional rather than toxicological: tannins chelate non-haem iron, and drinking tea with meals can measurably reduce iron absorption — relevant for people with iron-deficiency anaemia, who are advised to separate tea from meals by an hour or so.

Worth knowing

Tannins are what make strong tea, red wine and unripe fruit feel dry and puckering — the sensation is protein in your saliva being precipitated on your tongue, not a taste at all. The same protein-binding ability is what tanning leather is named after.
