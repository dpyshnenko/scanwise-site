What it is

Saccharin (E954) is the granddaddy of artificial sweeteners, discovered by accident in 1879. It is 300 to 400 times sweeter than sugar but leaves a notorious metallic or bitter aftertaste.

How it is used in food

Sweetener.

Commonly found in
  • Tabletop sweeteners (Sweet'N Low)
  • Diet sodas
  • Sugar-free jams and baked goods
  • Toothpaste and mouthwash

Health and safety

Carried a US cancer warning for 20 years until the rat-bladder mechanism was shown not to apply to humans.

Saccharin has survived decades of controversy. In the 1970s, studies linked it to bladder cancer in rats. For over 20 years, every product containing saccharin in the US had to carry a warning label stating it "may be hazardous to your health".

Metabolic concerns

general population · evidence: suspected

Like other artificial sweeteners, long-term use is not recommended by the WHO for weight control due to potential risks of type 2 diabetes.

WHO advice on non-sugar sweeteners

Delisted from carcinogens in 2000, but its long shadow remains.

Alternatives to this additive

  • E955: Sucralose — Provides a much cleaner, sugar-like taste without the bitter, metallic aftertaste of saccharin.

How it is made

Historically synthesized from coal tar derivatives, it is now produced via the reaction of phthalic anhydride or toluene with various chemicals.

  1. Synthesis — Synthesized from toluene or phthalic anhydride via multi-step chemical reactions.

Worth knowing

The oldest artificial sweetener (1879, discovered by a chemist who didn’t wash his hands before dinner).

Conclusion

In 2000, the warning labels were removed. Scientists discovered that the mechanism causing bladder cancer in rats was highly specific to rodent biology and did not apply to humans. The IARC formally downgraded it from a "possible human carcinogen" to "not classifiable". However, the WHO still advises against using it for long-term weight control.
