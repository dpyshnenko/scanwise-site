What it is

A mixture of polypeptides and amino acids formed by the partial hydrolysis of proteins.

How it is used in food

Peptone is protein broken down by enzymes or acid into short peptides and amino acids, used in food as a foaming and emulsifying aid and as a nitrogen source for fermentation — it feeds the yeasts and bacteria that make bread, beer, yoghurt and vinegar. Because it is already partly digested, it is also readily absorbed, which is why peptide-based protein hydrolysates are used in clinical and hypoallergenic nutrition.

Commonly found in
  • Nutrient media
  • Specialty foods

Health and safety

Safe for consumption, as it is just partially digested protein.

Peptones from food-grade sources are regarded as safe and are not subject to an ADI; the underlying material is ordinary protein, and hydrolysis is what happens in the gut anyway. Two practical caveats are well documented. First, source matters: peptone may be made from casein, meat, gelatine, soy or yeast, so vegans, vegetarians and people avoiding specific allergens need to know the origin, which is rarely stated on labels. Second, hydrolysates carry free glutamate and can contribute savoury 'umami' flavour, and enzymatic hydrolysis of allergenic proteins does not reliably remove allergenicity unless extensively performed and verified.

Worth knowing

Peptone is the workhorse of microbiology laboratories worldwide: nutrient agar, tryptone soy broth and most standard growth media are built on it, and food-safety testing for Salmonella or Listeria almost always starts by reviving the sample in buffered peptone water. The name dates to 1880s German physiology, from the Greek for 'to digest'.
