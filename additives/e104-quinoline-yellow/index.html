What it is

Quinoline Yellow (E104) is a bright, synthetic yellow-green food dye. It belongs to a group of colourants that have faced intense scrutiny over behavioural effects in children.

How it is used in food

Used for bright yellow coloration.

Commonly found in
  • Soft drinks and cordials
  • Sweets and chewing gum
  • Smoked haddock (historically)

Health and safety

Linked to hyperactivity in children.

E104 is one of the "Southampton Six" — a group of artificial dyes associated with increased hyperactivity and reduced attention span in children.

Hyperactivity in children

children · evidence: probable

Linked to increased hyperactivity and reduced attention in children in the 2007 Southampton trial, which tested it in a mixture with sodium benzoate. EFSA did not accept a causal link for the individual colour, but the EU legislated a mandatory warning label anyway.

Southampton study (Lancet 2007)

Restricted in the EU after the Southampton findings.

Alternatives to this additive

  • E100: Curcumin — A plant-derived yellow from turmeric with no hyperactivity warning attached, though it carries its own 3 mg/kg ADI.

How it is made

It is a petrochemical derivative synthesized from quinoline and phthalic anhydride.

  1. Synthesis — Produced chemically from coal tar derivatives.

Worth knowing

It is banned in food in the US, Canada, and Japan.

Conclusion

Due to the hyperactivity link, any food containing E104 in the EU must carry a mandatory warning label stating: "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children". Because of this, it is actively being phased out by most major food manufacturers in favour of natural colours like Curcumin (E100). It is completely banned in food in the United States and Japan.
