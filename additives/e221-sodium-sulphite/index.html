What it is

Sodium sulphite (E221) is a synthetic preservative and antioxidant. Like all sulphites, it releases active sulphur dioxide (E220) which prevents bacterial growth and stops food from turning brown.

How it is used in food

Strong antimicrobial and anti-browning power.

Commonly found in
  • Wine and cider
  • Dried fruits
  • Processed potatoes

Health and safety

Asthma sensitivity and mandatory allergen labeling.

For most people E221 causes no problems at the levels found in food. But sulphites must be declared on labels in the EU, US and Australia/New Zealand, because for one group of people they are genuinely dangerous.

Severe asthma trigger

asthmatics and sensitive individuals · evidence: established

Sulphites can trigger bronchospasms, hives, and anaphylaxis in sensitive individuals (roughly 5-10% of asthmatics).

EFSA re-evaluation of sulfur dioxide and sulfites

Shares the sulphite family's vitamin B1 destruction caution.

Alternatives to this additive

How it is made

Produced by reacting sulphur dioxide gas with a solution of alkali carbonates or hydroxides.

  1. Synthesis — Sulphur dioxide is absorbed in an aqueous alkaline solution.

Worth knowing

Releases the same active SO₂ as E220.

Conclusion

In roughly 5-10% of asthmatics, even trace amounts of sulphites can trigger bronchospasm, hives and, rarely, anaphylaxis — which is why they must be declared above 10 mg/kg. For everyone else the risk is far lower, though EFSA's 2016 re-evaluation described the safety data as inadequate, treated the 0.7 mg/kg ADI as provisional, and found high consumers exceeding it. Worth watching if you have asthma, worth not ignoring if you eat a lot of dried fruit and wine.
